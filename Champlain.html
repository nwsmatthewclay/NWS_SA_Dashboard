Interpretation & caveats

The mapped corridor is a wind-oriented hypothesis, not a calibrated probability. Future versions will combine lake-to-850-hPa temperature contrast, boundary-layer moisture, inversion/cap, directional shear, DGZ saturation, lake ice and radar/satellite evidence.

Lake Champlain research distinguishes isolated, synoptic-embedded and transitional bands. Wind shifts can relocate the favored corridor substantially.

06 / Research guide

Literature translated into a Champlain workflow

Research notes
Tardy (2000) · Lake effect and lake enhanced snow in the Champlain Valley

Local NWS technical attachment using Burlington Doppler radar, GOES imagery and high-resolution model output. Emphasizes that low clouds and flurries are common in Arctic outbreaks over the warmer lake, while significant lake-effect snow is less common.

Nyzio & Liu (2025) · Lake-Effect Snowfall Climatology over Lake Champlain

Analyzes 64 events in 2015–2024 and compares with 1997–2006. Separates isolated, synoptic and transitional bands; supports using local event types rather than importing Great Lakes thresholds unchanged.

Laird et al. (2009) · Climatology of Lake-Effect Precipitation Events over Lake Champlain

Identifies isolated lake-effect bands, quasi-stationary bands embedded in synoptic precipitation, and transitional cases. Radar morphology and synoptic context belong alongside thermodynamics.

NWS Buffalo research collection · lake-effect snow & BUFKIT

Great Lakes research contributes useful process understanding: lake–air temperature contrast, boundary-layer depth and capping, moisture, fetch, wind direction, band orientation and downstream terrain. The Lake Champlain implementation must account for its much smaller size and north–south geometry.

Ontario Winter Lake-effect Systems (OWLeS)

Research highlights how boundary-layer turbulence, microphysics, capping inversions, lake fluxes and shoreline/terrain interactions influence band intensity and model uncertainty.
